FOUNDING CHARTER & GOVERNANCE

A proposed framework for an inventor-support organization, with a paid employee-membership model, transparent commercialization agreements, and a 90% employee supermajority mechanism.

PROPOSED // NOT ADOPTED // NOT INCORPORATED BY THIS DOCUMENT

Version 0.2 • Draft for discussion • British Columbia, Canada • Updated 28 October 2026

// READ THIS FIRST

Document status and limits

These are proposals, not adopted legal documents. Publishing them does not incorporate Team Blacklight, create a society, establish employment, transfer intellectual property, or create royalty obligations. Do not use them as signed contracts or file them without appropriate review.

This page presents three connected work products in the requested order: (A) a preliminary legal and governance issue review, (C) a working constitution and bylaws draft, and (B) a polished founding charter. The constitution and bylaws are deliberately separate: under British Columbia's Societies Act, the constitution states the society's name and purposes, while the bylaws contain its internal governance rules.

Official references: British Columbia Societies Act and the Province of British Columbia's not-for-profit guidance. This is general drafting assistance, not legal, tax, accounting, or employment advice. Statutes and regulations can change; a BC lawyer should check the current law and the proposed organization's exact circumstances before adoption.

// A — PRELIMINARY REVIEW

Legal and governance review

Overall assessment: The framework has a coherent mission and useful safeguards, but several provisions need tighter drafting before they can be placed in bylaws or relied on in practice. The most important design issue is reconciling a workforce-wide voting model with statutory board responsibilities and the rule restricting the proportion of directors who may be paid by the society.

1. Board composition and paid employee-members

For an ordinary BC society, the Act generally requires at least three directors and at least one director ordinarily resident in BC. It also generally requires a majority of directors not to receive remuneration under employment or service contracts with the society (apart from remuneration for being a director). Directors must also satisfy statutory eligibility rules; the general minimum age is 18, subject to any applicable regulatory exception for 16- and 17-year-olds. These are statutory checks, not optional internal targets. See sections 40, 41 and 44 of the Act.

Design implication: employees can be voting members, but a board elected from a paid workforce must still maintain the required composition. The bylaws should require eligibility checks before election and throughout each director's term, with vacancies filled promptly. Do not assume that every employee can be a director or that an employee-majority board is automatically lawful.

2. Constitution versus bylaws

The constitution should contain only the society's name and purposes. Detailed rules about membership, elections, board meetings, voting, financial approvals, and the override belong in the bylaws or separately approved policies. Amendments to purposes and bylaws require the prescribed special-resolution and filing steps before they take legal effect. An informal online poll cannot replace those steps.

3. The 90% Employee Override

The override is the framework's defining governance idea, but it cannot lawfully operate as an unrestricted power to replace every board decision. The Act assigns directors responsibility to manage or supervise the society's activities and internal affairs and imposes duties to act honestly, in good faith, with appropriate care, and in accordance with the Act, regulations, and bylaws (sections 52–53). A member vote also cannot unilaterally amend a third-party contract or waive a director's legal duties.

Recommended implementation: define the override as a formal internal supermajority direction and mandatory reconsideration process. For matters reserved to members, require the proper member meeting or resolution; for bylaw or constitution changes, require the applicable special resolution and filing; for board-reserved matters, require the board to consider the direction promptly, document its decision, and implement it where lawful and within the board's authority. If the board cannot implement a direction, require a reasoned written explanation and lawful alternatives. This preserves the 90% threshold without promising a legally impossible result.

The proposed 90% denominator is valid votes cast by eligible employee-members, excluding abstentions. The bylaws must also define notice, quorum, voting method, ballot validation, conflicts, privacy, recounts, tie/threshold calculation, and a neutral way to resolve eligibility disputes. Because a 90% threshold is unusually high, consider a minimum participation/quorum rule to prevent a tiny number of votes from being treated as the will of the workforce.

4. Employee membership tied to employment

The Act allows membership to be admitted and terminated under the bylaws, but membership status and employment status are distinct legal questions. The bylaws should specify the effective date, notice, register update, resignation, disputed termination, and appeal/review process. They should not be used to avoid employment standards, wages owed, human-rights obligations, or contractual remedies. A membership dispute should not itself determine whether an employment termination was lawful.

5. Nonprofit operations and revenue

A society can earn revenue and pay reasonable compensation while applying its resources to its purposes, but it must not operate as a vehicle for distributing surplus to members. Commercial activity, grants, donations, restricted funding, payroll, GST/PST, income-tax treatment, and possible charitable registration should be reviewed with an accountant or tax professional. Incorporation as a society does not by itself make the organization a registered charity or authorize charitable tax receipts.

6. Intellectual property, royalties, and first refusal

The inventor-first approach is a good policy default, but ownership and permissions depend on the applicable law and the actual facts, including employee/contractor arrangements and pre-existing contributions. Each project should have a signed agreement before substantial work begins. It should identify background IP, newly created IP, licences or assignments, contributors, open-source dependencies, confidentiality, patent/public-disclosure decisions, manufacturing and distribution rights, royalty basis, audit/reporting rights, expenses, termination, inventory, and dispute resolution.

A right of first refusal needs precise trigger events, scope, response period, matching procedure, and what happens if the offer changes or the right-holder declines. Royalty terms should state whether the percentage is based on net sales, receipts, or another defined figure; list permitted deductions; address returns and taxes; set payment and reporting intervals; and avoid double-counting cost recovery. Have counsel review enforceability and fairness before using any template with an inventor.

7. Conflicts, records, privacy, and employment

The conflict policy should track the Act's director-interest rules, including disclosure and recusal where required, and extend clear procedures to staff and members. Member registers and governance records should be retained and accessed as the Act requires, while vote secrecy and applicant/inventor personal information should be protected. Establish retention periods, access controls, breach handling, and an appropriate privacy notice. Employment policies must account for BC employment standards, occupational health and safety, human rights, payroll, and any rules applying to young workers.

8. Dissolution and restricted assets

The dissolution clause should not promise that assets will simply be transferred to founders, staff, or members. Liabilities, restricted gifts, contracts, IP licences, royalty obligations, and statutory distribution rules must be addressed first. A lawyer should confirm that the final constitution and bylaws use appropriate asset-distribution wording for the selected society type.

Pre-incorporation checklist

  1. Confirm name availability and the exact society purposes.
  2. Confirm director eligibility and a compliant initial board.
  3. Have a BC lawyer review the constitution, bylaws, and override mechanism.
  4. Confirm tax, payroll, commercial activity, and charitable-status assumptions with an accountant.
  5. Draft and review employment, IP, confidentiality, royalty, manufacturing, and first-refusal agreements.
  6. Adopt financial controls, conflict rules, privacy practices, and recordkeeping procedures.
  7. Complete name reservation, incorporation filing, and ongoing reporting steps.

Bottom line: The 90% principle can be retained as a powerful internal governance commitment. It should be implemented as a defined process that triggers binding member action when legally available and otherwise requires documented reconsideration and lawful implementation—not as a blanket claim that an employee vote overrides the Act.

// C — WORKING LEGAL DRAFT

Proposed constitution

Drafting note: This is a short-form constitution draft, limited to the name and purposes as required by the BC Act. The name remains subject to name approval and registration.

1. Name

The name of the society is Team Blacklight (the “Society”), subject to approval and registration under applicable law.

2. Purposes

The purposes of the Society are:

  1. To support inventors and creators in researching, designing, developing, prototyping, testing, manufacturing, documenting, and commercializing useful products, technologies, and creative works.
  2. To improve access to technical knowledge, tools, equipment, expertise, mentorship, and collaborative resources for people developing inventions and products.
  3. To facilitate responsible research, engineering, product development, manufacturing readiness, quality assurance, and knowledge sharing.
  4. To establish collaborations and lawful commercial arrangements that advance these purposes, while respecting creators' intellectual-property rights and applying the Society's resources to its purposes.
  5. To obtain and manage donations, grants, sponsorships, earned revenue, equipment, and other resources consistent with the Society's purposes and applicable law.

The final purposes should be checked for legal suitability and consistency with the intended activities and tax treatment before filing.

// C — WORKING LEGAL DRAFT

Proposed bylaws

Status: Working draft for professional review. These clauses are not in force unless adopted through the required incorporation and governance procedures. Numbering and procedural details must be reconciled with the final filing forms and the Society's actual needs.

Part 1 — Interpretation and governing law

  1. In these bylaws, “Act” means the Societies Act of British Columbia and its regulations, as amended from time to time; “employee-member” means a voting member whose membership category is linked to current employment under these bylaws; and “Override Proposal” means a proposal submitted under Part 7.
  2. If a bylaw conflicts with the Act or another applicable law, the law prevails and the remaining bylaws continue to apply to the extent possible.
  3. The Society must apply its income and property to advancing its purposes. No member has a right to receive the Society's surplus merely by reason of membership.

Part 2 — Membership

  1. The Society has one voting class called “Employee-Members.” Each Employee-Member has one vote. No additional votes are granted because of title, seniority, salary, or department.
  2. A person becomes eligible for Employee-Membership when they begin paid employment with the Society and are admitted in the manner recorded in the membership register. The Society must give the person the current bylaws and explain membership rights and responsibilities.
  3. Membership linked to employment ends when employment ends, subject to the effective-date, notice, register-update, and review procedures in a membership policy adopted consistently with these bylaws and the Act. A dispute about employment or membership must be handled separately and fairly; neither status alone decides the other legal question.
  4. Members may resign in writing. Membership may be suspended or terminated only in accordance with these bylaws and applicable law, with reasonable notice of the grounds and an opportunity to respond where required.
  5. Inventors, contractors, donors, manufacturers, supporters, and other collaborators are not members solely because they work with or support the Society. Any additional membership class must be created by bylaw amendment and must state eligibility, rights, and obligations.
  6. The Society must maintain the membership register and other records required by law. Personal information must be handled under the Society's privacy and recordkeeping policies and applicable law.
  7. A member who disputes eligibility or a register entry may request a written review by a person or committee without a material conflict. The reviewer must provide a reasoned decision within a reasonable time, subject to any statutory rights and remedies.

Part 3 — General meetings and member voting

  1. The Society must hold annual and other general meetings as required by the Act. Meetings may be electronic where permitted by law and the bylaws, and notices must include participation and voting instructions.
  2. Notice, quorum, voting methods, proxies if permitted, and the procedure for ordinary and special resolutions must comply with the Act and these bylaws. A special resolution must meet the statutory definition and filing requirements.
  3. Each Employee-Member has one vote on matters on which members are entitled to vote. Abstentions are not votes cast where the applicable threshold is calculated by votes cast.
  4. Members may requisition a general meeting in accordance with the Act. Nothing in these bylaws removes or reduces a statutory member right.
  5. The Society may use secure electronic voting for internal proposals if the voting rules identify eligible voters, notice, authentication, closing time, ballot validity, privacy, audit records, and a process for challenging results. Electronic voting does not replace any formal meeting or resolution required by law.

Part 4 — Directors and board

  1. The Society must have at least the number of directors required by the Act, and at least one director must be ordinarily resident in British Columbia where the Act requires it. Director eligibility, age, disqualifications, and remuneration must comply with the Act and regulations.
  2. Directors are elected by voting members at a general meeting. The board should consist of three or more directors, with terms of two years, staggered where practical. The first directors and first-term arrangements must be set out in the incorporation records or an initial member resolution.
  3. A candidate must provide written consent and confirm eligibility before taking office. The secretary must record nominations, results, terms, resignations, vacancies, and changes required for filing.
  4. The board manages or supervises the Society's activities and internal affairs, subject to the Act, regulations, constitution, bylaws, and valid member resolutions. It approves the annual budget, financial controls, risk policies, material contracts, and delegated authorities.
  5. A majority of directors must not receive or be entitled to receive remuneration under employment or service contracts with the Society, except as the Act permits. The secretary and board must monitor compliance continuously. Employment as staff does not automatically qualify a person to serve as a director.
  6. The board may delegate day-to-day operations to employees, officers, committees, or senior managers, with written limits and reporting. Delegation does not remove statutory duties.
  7. A director must disclose material conflicts, abstain, leave the discussion where required, and refrain from improperly influencing a decision, consistent with the Act. The disclosure and handling must be recorded.
  8. The board must meet as needed. Notice, quorum, electronic attendance, written resolutions, minutes, and voting must comply with the Act and a board procedure approved by the directors. Directors may not vote by proxy where prohibited by law.
  9. Director removal, resignation, vacancy, replacement, and term expiry must follow the Act and these bylaws. Nothing here limits a statutory right to remove a director by special resolution.

Part 5 — Officers, staff, and delegated authority

  1. The board may appoint a chair, secretary, treasurer, operational lead, and other officers, and may define their responsibilities in a written policy. An officer's title alone does not confer director status or unlimited authority.
  2. Employees are engaged under separate employment agreements and policies. Membership rights do not replace wages, employment protections, contractual rights, or legal obligations.
  3. Only persons with documented authority may bind the Society, approve spending, sign contracts, or commit Society assets. Signing and spending limits must be recorded in a financial-control policy.

Part 6 — Finance, records, and conflicts

  1. The Society must maintain proper accounting records, financial statements, minutes, member and director records, and other records required by the Act. Financial statements must be approved as required by law.
  2. The board must approve an annual operating budget and a financial-control policy covering bank access, expense approvals, payroll, restricted funds, royalty liabilities, reimbursements, inventory, and reporting.
  3. The board must establish a documented threshold or criteria for material financial commitments requiring member approval. That policy cannot displace statutory authority or binding contractual obligations.
  4. Directors, officers, employees, and members must disclose material conflicts of interest. The Society must record disclosures and use recusal or independent review where required or appropriate.
  5. Donations and sponsorships may be accepted, restricted, refused, or returned only under an approved policy and applicable law. Donations do not automatically confer membership, votes, board seats, IP rights, or project control. The Society must not issue charitable donation receipts unless authorized to do so.
  6. The Society must adopt appropriate privacy, information-security, retention, workplace-safety, human-rights, and employment-compliance policies.

Part 7 — 90% Employee Override

  1. Purpose. The Employee Override gives Employee-Members a formal mechanism to require reconsideration and express a supermajority direction on an internal organizational decision.
  2. Scope. An Override Proposal may challenge an internal policy, operational or strategic decision, project selection, royalty policy, intellectual-property policy, or other matter within the Society's lawful authority. It cannot require unlawful conduct, extinguish third-party rights, waive statutory obligations, or remove directors' non-delegable duties.
  3. Initiation. Any Employee-Member may submit a written proposal identifying the decision challenged, the replacement decision sought, reasons, and known financial, operational, contractual, or legal consequences. The secretary must acknowledge receipt and arrange an eligibility and completeness check within a reasonable time.
  4. Notice and ballot. Eligible Employee-Members must receive the full proposal, neutral explanatory material, voting period, method, eligibility cutoff, and process for raising a challenge. A secure ballot must permit one vote per eligible Employee-Member and protect ballot secrecy to the extent practical while preserving an auditable count.
  5. Threshold. An Override Proposal passes only if votes in favour are at least 90% of valid votes cast. Abstentions and invalid ballots are excluded from votes cast. The official record must show the eligible population, ballots received, valid votes for and against, abstentions, invalid ballots, and calculation. A minimum participation threshold and independent ballot verification must be set in an approved voting procedure before the first vote.
  6. Conflicts and challenges. A person whose eligibility or conduct is challenged must have a fair opportunity to respond. A person with a material conflict must disclose it; any recusal from a particular vote must be based on an objective, pre-existing rule and not selectively imposed to affect the outcome. A neutral reviewer should decide ballot challenges and preserve a written record.
  7. Effect. A successful vote is a formal internal direction and triggers prompt review and implementation to the fullest extent lawful. If the matter is reserved to members, the Society must convene the required meeting or conduct the legally prescribed resolution process. If it concerns a constitution or bylaw amendment, the required special resolution and filing must be completed. If it concerns a matter within board authority, the board must consider the direction promptly and record its decision and implementation plan.
  8. Board decision and legal limits. The Override does not automatically replace a director decision where the Act assigns responsibility to the board, does not compel directors to breach their duties, and does not amend a third-party contract. If the board concludes that the proposal cannot be implemented as written, it must issue a written explanation identifying the specific legal, contractual, or governance obstacle and any lawful alternative that substantially respects the vote.
  9. Timing and records. The board or responsible officer must publish an implementation status update within 30 days after certification, unless the matter requires a different statutory process or a documented extension. The Society must preserve the proposal, notices, voting eligibility basis, tally, certification, decision, explanation, and implementation records, with appropriate privacy safeguards.
  10. Non-retaliation. No person may retaliate against a member or employee solely for participating in a properly conducted Override vote or expressing a good-faith governance view. This does not immunize unrelated misconduct or override lawful employment requirements.
  11. Review. The board must review the procedure at least annually with employee-member input. Any amendment to these bylaws must follow the statutory bylaw-amendment process; a vote under this Part cannot bypass it.

Part 8 — Intellectual property and commercialization

  1. The Society must not assume ownership of an inventor's pre-existing intellectual property merely because it provides advice, equipment, prototyping, funding, or other support.
  2. Before substantial work begins, a written project agreement should define background IP, newly created IP, ownership, licences, manufacturing/distribution permissions, confidentiality, expenses, royalties, reporting, termination, inventory, and dispute resolution.
  3. Employee, contractor, and collaborator ownership rights must be addressed in signed agreements and under applicable law. No blanket assignment or licence is created by these bylaws alone.
  4. The board may approve a standard royalty policy and model agreements. Project-specific terms must be documented and signed by authorized parties before the terms apply.
  5. A right of first refusal or other first opportunity is effective only where a separate written agreement defines the covered rights, trigger, response period, matching method, scope, and consequences of declining or not responding.

Part 9 — Amendments and dissolution

  1. These bylaws may be amended only through the special-resolution and filing process required by the Act. Constitution amendments must follow the applicable special-resolution and filing requirements.
  2. On dissolution, the Society must deal with liabilities, restricted funds, contracts, IP rights, royalty obligations, and remaining property under the Act and applicable agreements. No member is entitled to surplus assets merely because of membership.

Professional review required: This is a working outline, not a ready-to-file bylaw set. A BC lawyer should review the exact statutory language, voting thresholds and procedures, director qualifications and remuneration, membership termination, meeting notice and quorum, financial controls, and dissolution provisions before adoption.

// B — POLISHED FOUNDING DOCUMENT

Team Blacklight Founding Charter and Governance Framework

Status: Proposed founding document • Jurisdiction: British Columbia, Canada • Version: 0.2 — Draft for discussion

1. Organizational identity

Name. The proposed organization will operate as Team Blacklight, subject to name approval and registration requirements.

Purpose. Team Blacklight aims to help inventors turn ideas into real, usable products by supporting design, research, development, prototyping, manufacturing, and commercialization. It seeks to make invention more accessible to people who may lack the resources, equipment, expertise, or organizational support to pursue their ideas independently.

Principles. Team Blacklight is guided by innovation, accessibility, respect for inventor ownership, fair compensation, financial sustainability, independence, democratic participation, transparency, and responsible development.

Nonprofit character. The Society will apply its revenue and resources to its purposes and legitimate operating needs rather than distribute surplus to members. It may earn revenue, receive donations, enter commercial agreements, and pay employees. Membership alone does not entitle a person to the Society's surplus. Nonprofit incorporation does not by itself confer registered-charity status.

2. Activities and services

Consistent with its purposes and applicable law, Team Blacklight may help refine concepts and product designs; support engineering research; build and test prototypes; provide access to tools, equipment, software, and expertise; coordinate manufacturing; assess production feasibility and costs; support documentation and quality assurance; assist commercialization and distribution; build partnerships; seek compatible donations, grants, and sponsorships; and develop internal technologies that advance its mission.

3. Organizational structure

The proposed model distinguishes employee-members, the board of directors, operational leadership, external inventors and collaborators, and supporters or donors. Employment does not automatically make a person a director. Working on a project or making a donation does not automatically confer membership, voting rights, or ownership.

4. Employee membership

Under the proposed bylaws, eligible paid employees become voting Employee-Members through the defined admission process. Each has one vote regardless of job title, seniority, salary, department, or length of service. Membership linked to employment ends according to the bylaws when employment ends, with a fair process for disputed eligibility and accurate register updates. Membership termination does not cancel outstanding wages, contractual rights, intellectual-property rights, or other legal obligations. External collaborators do not become voting members automatically.

5. Board of directors

The board oversees the Society's finances, legal compliance, policies, employment practices, risk, budgets, assets, commercial agreements, and implementation of valid member decisions. Directors are elected under the bylaws and must meet the Act's eligibility, residency, composition, remuneration, and duty requirements. The board may delegate operations, but delegation does not remove statutory responsibilities.

6. Ordinary decision-making

Authorized staff and project leads may make routine operational decisions within approved policies and budgets. The board manages or supervises the Society subject to the Act, bylaws, and valid member decisions. Members elect directors and decide matters reserved to them by law or the bylaws. Major changes and other reserved matters must follow the formal resolution and filing procedures that apply.

7. The 90% Employee Override

Team Blacklight proposes a supermajority mechanism allowing any Employee-Member to submit a documented challenge to an internal decision. The proposal must identify the decision, replacement sought, reasons, and known consequences. It passes when at least 90% of valid votes cast by eligible Employee-Members support it; abstentions are excluded. The voting procedure must provide clear notice, eligibility verification, secure and auditable ballots, challenge handling, privacy, and a defined participation threshold.

A successful vote creates a formal internal direction and requires prompt consideration and implementation to the fullest extent lawful. Where a formal member resolution, board decision, filing, contractual consent, or other legal step is required, that step must still occur. An override cannot require unlawful conduct, remove non-delegable director duties, or cancel third-party rights. If the board cannot implement a proposal as written, it must explain the specific obstacle in writing and identify lawful alternatives that substantially respect the employees' decision. Participation in a properly conducted vote must not itself trigger retaliation.

8. Intellectual property

Unless a written agreement says otherwise, an external inventor retains their pre-existing intellectual property. Team Blacklight will not claim ownership merely because it provides advice, tools, prototyping assistance, manufacturing support, or funding. Written project agreements should define background and newly created IP, ownership, licences, manufacturing and distribution rights, confidentiality, expenses, royalties, termination, inventory, and dispute resolution. Employee, contractor, and collaborator contributions must be addressed by appropriate agreements and applicable law.

9. Royalties and commercialization

Royalties may help support production costs, operations, and future development. Team Blacklight may also agree to fees or cost reimbursement. A standard schedule may be adopted by policy, while project-specific rates may reflect costs, risk, funding, distribution duties, complexity, expected sales, inventor circumstances, and the support provided. Every agreement must define the royalty basis, deductions, taxes, refunds, reporting, payment dates, and cost recovery. No particular percentage is established by this charter. Inventors retain commercialization rights except where a binding written agreement provides otherwise.

10. Right of first refusal

A first refusal or other first opportunity applies only if a written project agreement defines the covered product or rights, trigger, proposal or offer, response period, matching terms, scope, and consequences of declining or failing to respond. It does not itself transfer ownership of intellectual property.

11. Donations and sponsorships

Team Blacklight may accept compatible donations and sponsorships and may refuse contributions that create unacceptable conflicts, inappropriate control, legal or financial risk, or mission inconsistency. Restricted contributions must be documented and used according to accepted conditions and law. Donations do not automatically grant membership, votes, board seats, IP rights, project control, or preferential commercial treatment. Charitable tax receipts may be issued only if the organization is legally authorized to do so.

12. Financial management

Potential revenue includes royalties, development and manufacturing agreements, technical services, grants, donations, sponsorships, and other lawful mission-consistent activities. The board will approve a budget and controls for restricted funding, project costs, royalty liabilities, payroll, expenses, and operating costs. Major financial commitments requiring member approval must be defined in a written policy. Compensation and director payments must comply with law, bylaws, and conflict rules.

13. Conflicts of interest

Directors, officers, employees, and members must disclose material conflicts connected with Society decisions. The Society will document disclosures and apply independent review, recusal, and other safeguards as required by law and policy. A member vote does not excuse a conflict or authorize unlawful conduct.

14. Employment and workplace standards

The Society will use appropriate employment agreements, role descriptions, payroll procedures, and workplace policies. It will comply with applicable wage, working-time, payroll, safety, human-rights, and young-worker requirements. Membership voting rights do not replace employment rights or legal protections.

15. Amendments and policy changes

The constitution and bylaws will set out the applicable formal amendment procedures. Employee-Members may use the override process to propose policy changes and initiate the proper process for fundamental changes. Required resolutions, filings, and third-party consents must be completed before a change is treated as legally effective. Significant changes to purposes, voting rights, IP policy, or royalties should be documented and communicated.

16. Dissolution

On dissolution, remaining property must be handled under the constitution, bylaws, applicable contracts, and BC law. Assets must not simply be distributed to employees or members because they hold membership. Debts, restricted donations, IP rights, royalty obligations, and third-party commitments must be addressed through the proper process.

17. Adoption and implementation

This charter is a proposed framework, not a substitute for the legal documents needed to establish a society. Before incorporation, the founders should confirm the name and purposes; identify eligible initial directors; prepare the constitution and bylaws; review the employee-membership and override model; develop royalty and project-agreement templates; prepare employment, confidentiality, and IP agreements; establish financial and recordkeeping controls; obtain legal and accounting advice; and complete the required registration and ongoing compliance steps.

Proposed governing principle: Team Blacklight will be operated to advance its purposes, with a paid employee-membership model that gives its workforce a meaningful democratic voice. The board and leadership will act within their lawful authority, while a 90% employee supermajority can initiate an override of internal decisions and require the Society to pursue implementation to the fullest extent legally possible.
